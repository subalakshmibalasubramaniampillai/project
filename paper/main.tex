\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

People with diabetes are admitted to hospital more often than people
without it, and a notable share of those admissions is followed by an
early return \citep{rubin2015}. Unplanned readmission within 30 days is
costly, it is used as a quality indicator, and it often reflects problems
that began before discharge: an unstable glycaemic regimen, unresolved
co-morbidity, or a care transition that was not prepared. A second, less
studied signal is the escalation of glucose-lowering therapy between
consecutive stays. Treatment intensification is the expected response to
worsening glycaemic control, and its timing is known to lag behind clinical
need \citep{khunti2013}; anticipating it at discharge could help teams plan
follow-up and medication review \citep{ada2024}.

Both outcomes depend on information that is spread over time and over a
large vocabulary of clinical codes. Hospital records store diagnoses as
ICD-9-CM codes, and those codes are organised in a public hierarchy that
groups related conditions. Many codes are rare in any single institution,
so a model that learns one free parameter vector per code has little to go
on for most of them. At the same time, the history available for a given
patient is short and uneven: in routinely collected inpatient data, most
patients appear only once, while a minority accumulate many stays. A
predictive model therefore has to decide, patient by patient, how much the
past should change the estimate derived from the current stay.

Most published readmission models for the widely used Diabetes 130-US
Hospitals database \citep{strack2014} treat each encounter as an
independent row, discarding the link between stays of the same person.
Deep sequence models developed for large EHR corpora, such as recurrent
networks \citep{choi2016doctorai}, reverse-time attention
\citep{choi2016retain} or Transformers \citep{li2020behrt,rasmy2021medbert},
do use this link, and ontology-aware representations such as GRAM
\citep{choi2017gram} and KAME \citep{ma2018kame} address rare codes. Two
practical gaps remain. First, on moderate-sized tabular clinical data,
gradient-boosted decision trees are very strong and frequently match or
outperform neural networks \citep{grinsztajn2022}; new neural methods are
seldom compared against well-tuned boosting under identical inputs.
Second, evaluations often rely on a single random split at the encounter
level, which allows the same patient to appear in training and test data
and gives no estimate of variability, calibration or subgroup behaviour
\citep{collins2015tripod,vancalster2019}.

This paper addresses both gaps on real, publicly available hospital data.
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A temporal knowledge-gated network (TKGN).} TKGN encodes
  each encounter with a hybrid clinical knowledge graph that combines the
  ICD-9-CM hierarchy with a co-morbidity graph estimated from training
  encounters, summarises the patient's earlier stays, and fuses the past
  with the current stay through \emph{history-reliability gates} that are
  conditioned on the amount of history and on the change since the last
  stay.
  \item \textbf{A boosting-anchored variant (TKGN-B).} Instead of competing
  with gradient boosting, TKGN-B learns a residual on top of an
  out-of-fold LightGBM logit, so that the network only has to explain what
  the tree ensemble misses, chiefly the longitudinal and relational
  structure.
  \item \textbf{Two clinically motivated tasks on the same cohort}:
  30-day readmission and escalation of glucose-lowering treatment at the
  next stay.
  \item \textbf{A leakage-controlled evaluation protocol} with
  patient-disjoint repeated splits, a prospective temporal split, strong
  tuned baselines (five tabular learners and three sequence models),
  patient-clustered bootstrap confidence intervals, DeLong tests,
  calibration analysis, component ablations, learning curves and a
  subgroup fairness audit. All code and derived results are released.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Readmission prediction in diabetes}
Systematic reviews report that most readmission models reach only
moderate discrimination, with areas under the ROC curve commonly between
0.6 and 0.7 \citep{kansagara2011}. The Diabetes 130-US Hospitals database
was released together with an analysis of HbA1c measurement and
readmission \citep{strack2014} and has since become a common benchmark.
Readmission risk in diabetes is associated with prior utilisation,
co-morbidity burden and discharge circumstances \citep{rubin2015}; these
factors are recorded across stays, which motivates sequence-aware
modelling.

\paragraph{Sequence models for EHR}
Recurrent networks were among the first deep models applied to visit
sequences \citep{choi2016doctorai}. RETAIN introduced two-level attention
over reversed visit sequences to improve interpretability
\citep{choi2016retain}, and Transformer encoders pre-trained on large
claims or EHR corpora have since been proposed
\citep{li2020behrt,rasmy2021medbert}. Large-scale studies showed that deep
models trained on complete EHR can predict readmission and other outcomes
\citep{rajkomar2018}. Surveys document the breadth of this work and its
reliance on large proprietary data \citep{shickel2018deepehr}; public
multitask benchmarks exist mainly for intensive-care time series
\citep{harutyunyan2019}.

\paragraph{Knowledge-aware code representations}
GRAM represents a medical code as an attention-weighted combination of
its ancestors in a clinical ontology and showed gains for rare codes
\citep{choi2017gram}; KAME extends this idea by using knowledge at the
prediction stage \citep{ma2018kame}. Graph convolution
\citep{kipf2017} and co-occurrence statistics such as pointwise mutual
information \citep{church1990} are standard tools for relational
structure. TKGN differs from these works in three respects: it keeps a
code-specific embedding and adds ancestor information residually rather
than as a convex combination; it refines category embeddings with an
empirical co-morbidity graph whose propagation weights are initialised at
zero; and it couples the code encoder with an explicit, history-length
aware gating of longitudinal information.

\paragraph{Trees, networks and hybrids}
Gradient-boosted trees \citep{friedman2001,chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm}
remain the reference for heterogeneous tabular data
\citep{grinsztajn2022}. Hybrid designs that feed tree outputs into another
learner \citep{he2014facebook} or combine learners by stacking
\citep{wolpert1992} are well established. TKGN-B uses the boosting logit
as a fixed offset and trains the network on the residual, with
out-of-fold offsets on the training data to avoid optimistic anchoring.
Gating as a fusion mechanism has been studied for multimodal inputs
\citep{arevalo2017gmu}; here the gate arbitrates between the current stay
and an uncertain, variable-length history.

\paragraph{Evaluation of clinical prediction models}
Reporting guidelines ask for calibration, internal validation that
respects the data structure, and transparent handling of thresholds
\citep{collins2015tripod}. Poor calibration can make an accurate model
harmful \citep{vancalster2019}, and modern neural networks are often
mis-calibrated \citep{guo2017}. Algorithmic bias across racial groups has
been documented in deployed risk tools \citep{obermeyer2019}, which
motivates subgroup audits.

\section{Data and cohort}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Source}
We used the Diabetes 130-US Hospitals for Years 1999--2008 database
\citep{strack2014,uci296}, distributed by the UCI Machine Learning
Repository under a CC BY 4.0 licence. It contains \NRaw{} de-identified
inpatient encounters of patients with diabetes from 130 US hospitals and
integrated delivery networks. Each encounter lasted one to fourteen days
and includes demographics, admission and discharge characteristics,
utilisation counts for the preceding year, up to three ICD-9-CM
diagnoses, HbA1c and serum glucose test results, the prescription status
of 23 glucose-lowering drugs (not prescribed, steady, dose increased, dose
decreased) and the readmission outcome. A persistent patient number links
repeat encounters of the same person. The file used in this study is the
unmodified public release; its SHA-256 checksum is verified by the code
before any processing. No record was simulated, imputed from synthetic
distributions or augmented.

\subsection{Cohort definition}
Following \citet{strack2014}, encounters ending in death or discharge to
hospice were excluded because readmission is not possible after them;
three encounters with unknown sex were also removed
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cohort}). The resulting cohort has \NEnc{} encounters of
\NPat{} patients, of whom \NRepeat{} have two or more encounters.
Encounters of a patient were ordered by encounter identifier, which
increases with admission time in this release (calendar dates are not
distributed). \NHist{} encounters (\PctHist\%) have at least one earlier
stay. Table~\ref{tab:cohort} summarises the cohort.

\subsection{Prediction tasks}
Each sample is an \emph{index encounter} $e_t$ of a patient. The model may
use $e_t$ and the patient's strictly earlier encounters
$e_{t-L},\dots,e_{t-1}$ ($L=10$), but never the outcome of $e_t$ or any
later encounter.

\emph{Task 1, 30-day readmission.} The label is positive if the patient
was readmitted within 30 days of discharge from $e_t$ (all \NEnc{}
encounters; prevalence \RateReadmit\%).

\emph{Task 2, treatment escalation at the next stay.} For encounters that
are followed by another encounter of the same patient (\NEsc{} samples),
the label is positive if the next encounter shows intensification of
glucose-lowering therapy: insulin started when it was not prescribed at
$e_t$, any drug with an increased dose, or a larger number of concurrently
prescribed glucose-lowering drugs than at $e_t$ (prevalence \RateEsc\%).
Treatment intensification is a recognised marker of deteriorating
glycaemic control \citep{khunti2013}.

The readmission outcome of an \emph{earlier} encounter is known when the
index encounter takes place (the readmission, if any, occurred before or
at the index admission) and is therefore a legitimate input. The outcome
of the index encounter is replaced by an ``unknown'' token for every
model.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

Fig.~\ref{fig:framework} gives an overview. We first describe the
knowledge graph, then the encounter encoder shared by all neural models,
the temporal module and gates of TKGN, and finally the boosting-anchored
variant TKGN-B.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_framework.pdf}
  \caption{Overview of TKGN and TKGN-B. Every encounter is encoded with
  knowledge-grounded code embeddings; earlier encounters are summarised
  and combined with the index encounter through history-reliability
  gates. TKGN-B adds the network output to an out-of-fold gradient
  boosting logit.}
  \label{fig:framework}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Hybrid clinical knowledge graph}
\label{sec:kg}
Let $\mathcal{V}$ be the set of concept nodes. \emph{Ontology edges}
connect each ICD-9-CM code to its four-character subcategory, its
three-character category, its chapter (17 numeric chapters plus the V and
E supplementary classifications) and a root node; a level is skipped when
it would repeat the text of the next level. Glucose-lowering drugs are
linked to their pharmacological classes (biguanide, sulfonylurea,
meglitinide, thiazolidinedione, alpha-glucosidase inhibitor, insulin).
A node is retained only if it is observed in at least five training
encounters, so that rare or unseen codes are represented through their
closest retained ancestors.

\emph{Co-morbidity edges} connect three-character categories $i,j$ that
co-occur in the same training encounter more often than expected. With
$n_{ij}$ the number of training encounters listing both categories and
$N$ the number of training encounters,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PPMI}(i,j) = \max\!\Big(0,\ \log \frac{n_{ij}\,N}{n_i\,n_j}\Big),
\end{equation}
computed only for pairs with $n_{ij}\ge 20$ and pruned to the ten strongest
partners of each node. The symmetric normalised adjacency with self-loops
is $\hat{\vect{A}} = \vect{D}^{-1/2}(\vect{P}+\vect{I})\vect{D}^{-1/2}$,
where $\vect{P}$ holds the PPMI weights. Both edge types are estimated from
the training partition only.

\subsection{Encounter encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}
\paragraph{Knowledge-grounded code embeddings}
Let $\vect{E}\in\R^{|\mathcal{V}|\times d}$ be free node embeddings. One
propagation step over the co-morbidity graph gives
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\vect{E}} = \vect{E} + \tanh\!\big(\hat{\vect{A}}\,\vect{E}\,\vect{W}_c\big),
  \qquad \vect{W}_c \leftarrow \vect{0}\ \text{at initialisation},
\end{equation}
so the graph changes a representation only when training finds it
useful. For a code $c$ with retained own node $v_c$ (if any) and proper
ancestors $\mathcal{A}(c)$,
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_{c,a} = \operatorname{softmax}_{a\in\mathcal{A}(c)}
    \Big(\vect{w}^\top \tanh\big(\vect{W}_\alpha[\tilde{\vect{e}}_{v_c};\tilde{\vect{e}}_a] + \vect{b}_\alpha\big)\Big),
  \qquad
  \vect{g}_c = \tilde{\vect{e}}_{v_c} + \sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}(c)} \alpha_{c,a}\,\tilde{\vect{e}}_a ,
\end{equation}
with $\tilde{\vect{e}}_{v_c}=\vect{0}$ for codes without a retained node.
Unlike the convex combination of GRAM \citep{choi2017gram}, the residual
form keeps frequent codes specific while rare codes borrow strength from
the hierarchy. The three diagnosis slots of an encounter receive learned
position embeddings and are pooled by attention.

\paragraph{Medications} Each drug $k$ is a token
$\vect{u}_k + \vect{R}^\top\vect{p}_k + \vect{s}_{\sigma_k}$, where
$\vect{p}_k$ is the (normalised) class-membership vector, $\vect{R}$ holds
class embeddings and $\sigma_k\in\{\text{steady, up, down}\}$ is the
prescription state. Active drugs are averaged; a learned vector represents
``no glucose-lowering drug''.

\paragraph{Administrative, demographic and laboratory fields}
Twelve categorical fields (race, sex, age band, admission type, discharge
disposition, admission source, payer, specialty, HbA1c result, glucose
result, medication change, diabetes medication) are embedded
individually ($d_f=16$) and concatenated. The eight counts (length of
stay, laboratory procedures, procedures, medications, outpatient,
emergency and inpatient visits in the prior year, number of diagnoses)
are represented by quantile-bin embeddings together with their
log-transformed standardised value \citep{gorishniy2022}. All vocabularies,
bin edges and scaling constants are fitted on training encounters.

\paragraph{Outcome token} For earlier encounters the known readmission
outcome (none, after 30 days, within 30 days) is embedded; the index
encounter always receives the ``unknown'' token. The five parts are
concatenated and mapped by a two-layer perceptron with layer
normalisation to $\vect{h}\in\R^{d}$.

\subsection{Temporal knowledge-gated network}
\label{sec:tkgn}
Let $\vect{h}_t$ be the index encounter and
$\vect{H}=[\vect{h}_{t-n_t},\dots,\vect{h}_{t-1}]$ the $n_t\le L$ earlier
encounters. TKGN computes three views of the past.

\emph{History summary.} A GRU \citep{cho2014gru} run over $\vect{H}$
yields its last state $\vect{s}_{\text{gru}}$. Recency-aware attention uses
the index encounter as query:
\begin{equation}
  \beta_j = \operatorname{softmax}_j\!\left(\frac{(\vect{W}_q\vect{h}_t)^\top
     \vect{W}_k(\vect{h}_j + \vect{r}_{t-j})}{\sqrt{d}}\right),\qquad
  \vect{s}_{\text{att}} = \sum_j \beta_j \vect{h}_j ,
\end{equation}
where $\vect{r}_{t-j}$ embeds how many stays ago encounter $j$ occurred.
The summary is $\vect{s}=\vect{W}_s[\vect{s}_{\text{gru}};\vect{s}_{\text{att}}]$,
or a learned vector $\vect{s}_\varnothing$ when $n_t=0$.

\emph{Change since the last stay.}
$\boldsymbol{\delta} = \vect{h}_t - \vect{h}_{t-1}$ ($\vect{0}$ if $n_t=0$)
captures escalation of treatment, utilisation or diagnostic burden
between consecutive stays.

\emph{History-reliability gates.} With $\boldsymbol{\ell}(n_t)$ an
embedding of the history length and
$\vect{c}=[\vect{h}_t;\vect{s};\boldsymbol{\delta};\boldsymbol{\ell}(n_t)]$,
\begin{equation}
  \vect{g}_s = \sigma(\vect{W}_{gs}\vect{c}+\vect{b}_{gs}),\quad
  \vect{g}_\delta = \sigma(\vect{W}_{g\delta}\vect{c}+\vect{b}_{g\delta}),\quad
  \vect{z} = \vect{h}_t + \vect{g}_s\odot\vect{s}
           + \vect{g}_\delta\odot\vect{W}_\delta\boldsymbol{\delta}.
\end{equation}
The gates are feature-wise and depend on the patient: a single earlier
stay with an unremarkable course can be down-weighted, while a long
history of early readmissions can dominate the representation.

\emph{Outputs and loss.} A two-layer head maps $\vect{z}$ to the risk
logit $f(\vect{z})$. An auxiliary linear head predicts the three-level
readmission outcome of the index stay (none, $>30$ days, $<30$ days), which
provides a richer training signal than the binary label
\citep{caruana1997}. The training objective is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L} = \mathrm{BCE}\big(y, \sigma(f(\vect{z}))\big)
   + \lambda\,\mathrm{CE}\big(o, \operatorname{softmax}(\vect{W}_o\vect{z})\big),
   \qquad \lambda = 0.5 .
\end{equation}
The auxiliary head is used only during training.

\subsection{Boosting-anchored TKGN (TKGN-B)}
\label{sec:tkgnb}
Gradient boosting captures threshold effects in counts and categorical
interactions very efficiently, whereas TKGN brings longitudinal and
relational structure. TKGN-B combines them additively on the logit scale:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{logit} P(y=1\mid e_{\le t}) = \phi(\vect{x}_t) + f(\vect{z}_t),
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is a LightGBM model \citep{ke2017lightgbm} trained on the
tabular view $\vect{x}_t$ of the same information (Section~\ref{sec:baselines})
and $f$ is the TKGN head. $\phi$ is kept fixed while TKGN is trained.
Using in-sample boosting logits as offsets would give the network a
target that is already over-fitted, so the training offsets are
\emph{out-of-fold}: training patients are split into five groups, and
the offset of each training encounter comes from a LightGBM model fit on
the other four groups with the hyper-parameters and number of trees
selected on the validation set. Validation and test offsets come from the
LightGBM model fit on all training patients. The network therefore learns
a residual correction, conceptually one additional boosting stage
\citep{friedman2001} implemented by a sequence model.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Validation designs}
\emph{Patient-grouped split.} Patients, not encounters, were randomly
assigned to training (70\%), validation (10\%) and test (20\%)
partitions, stratified by whether a patient has any positive sample, so
that no person contributes to more than one partition. The procedure was
repeated five times with different partitions and model seeds. For the
first repeat the partition sizes are \NReadmitGrpTrain{} / \NReadmitGrpVal{}
/ \NReadmitGrpTest{} encounters (readmission) and \NEscGrpTrain{} /
\NEscGrpVal{} / \NEscGrpTest{} (escalation).

\emph{Temporal (prospective) split.} Encounters were ordered by identifier
and cut at the 70th and 80th percentiles: models were fitted on the
earliest 70\%, tuned on the next 10\% and tested on the most recent 20\%
of admissions. A test encounter may belong to a patient seen earlier, as
in deployment, but its history only contains earlier stays. For the
escalation task an index encounter is kept only if its next encounter
falls in the same period, so no label is derived from a later period.
Three seeds were run.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
\emph{Tabular learners} use a design matrix with the eight counts,
one-hot categorical fields, drug states, multi-hot three-character
diagnosis categories (at least 50 training occurrences), chapter
indicators and hand-crafted history aggregates computed from strictly
earlier stays (number of earlier stays, earlier readmissions within and
after 30 days, the outcome of the last stay, mean length of stay,
medications, diagnoses and laboratory procedures over earlier stays,
earlier insulin use, and changes since the last stay); 524 features in
total. We trained L2-regularised logistic regression, random forest
\citep{breiman2001}, XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM
\citep{ke2017lightgbm} and a multilayer perceptron. \emph{Sequence
models} use the same encounter encoder as TKGN with flat code embeddings:
a GRU over the encounter sequence in the spirit of Doctor AI
\citep{choi2016doctorai}, RETAIN \citep{choi2016retain} and a two-layer
Transformer encoder \citep{vaswani2017}. All sequence baselines receive
the outcomes of earlier stays, exactly like TKGN.

\subsection{Training and model selection}
Hyper-parameters of the tabular learners were selected on the validation
partition from small grids (regularisation strength; minimum leaf size;
tree depth and minimum child weight; number of leaves and minimum child
samples; hidden sizes and weight decay). Boosting used a learning rate of
0.03 with early stopping on validation AUROC (up to 2{,}000 trees). All
neural models used $d=64$, dropout 0.3, AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019} with
learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-3}$, batch size 512,
gradient clipping at 1.0, and early stopping on validation AUROC (patience
4, at most 30 epochs). Architectural choices for the neural models
(quantile-bin and per-field embeddings in the shared encoder, the additive
code encoder, the auxiliary head of TKGN, regularisation) were made during
development on a separate random patient partition of the same cohort,
mainly using its validation data, and were frozen before the evaluation
runs reported here. Because all partitions are drawn from one cohort, some
patients used for development also appear in evaluation test sets; this may
bias the neural models slightly upwards and is discussed as a limitation.
Within the evaluation runs, the test partitions were used only for the
final assessment. Models were implemented in PyTorch
\citep{paszke2019} and scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011}.

\subsection{Metrics and statistical analysis}
Discrimination was measured by the area under the ROC curve (AUROC,
primary) and the area under the precision--recall curve (AUPRC).
Calibration was measured by the Brier score, the expected calibration
error over ten equal-width bins (ECE, \citealp{naeini2015}) and the
calibration slope and intercept from logistic recalibration
\citep{vancalster2019}. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive
value and F1 were computed at a threshold that maximised F1 on the
validation partition and was then frozen. For the grouped design we
report mean and standard deviation over the five repeats. For the first
test run of each design we computed 95\% confidence intervals with a
patient-clustered percentile bootstrap (1{,}000 resamples; patients
rather than encounters are resampled, \citealp{efron1993}), paired
bootstrap intervals for AUROC differences, and the DeLong test for
correlated AUROCs \citep{delong1988} using the fast algorithm of
\citet{sun2014}. The DeLong test treats encounters as independent and is
therefore reported together with the clustered bootstrap. Across the five
grouped repeats we also report how often a model won and a paired
$t$-test on per-repeat AUROC differences.

\subsection{Ablation, data efficiency, explanation and fairness}
Seven TKGN variants, each removing one component, were trained on the
first \NAblRuns{} grouped repeats. To test the pre-specified hypothesis
that knowledge-graph code sharing matters most when data are scarce, TKGN
and TKGN without the knowledge graph were additionally retrained on 5, 10
and 25\% of the training patients in the first three grouped repeats (paired
subsets and seeds). Learning curves retrained LightGBM,
GRU, TKGN and TKGN-B on 5, 10, 25 and 50\% of the training patients of
the first repeat with the same test patients. Global explanations were
obtained by permutation importance of input groups for TKGN (AUROC drop
over three permutations) and exact TreeSHAP values \citep{lundberg2020}
for the LightGBM anchor of TKGN-B. Subgroup performance was examined by
race, sex, age band, number of earlier stays and HbA1c testing
\citep{obermeyer2019}.
